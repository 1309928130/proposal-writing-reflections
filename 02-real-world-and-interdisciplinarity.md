## 3. Connecting to the real world, and to space / design

In my coaching, I often see students whose research question revolves around some
abstract concept or method, with weak relevance to the real world. Good research
questions usually need to articulate the problem's real-world significance. Even for
purely theoretical research, it is best to begin with one or two small stories from
the real world and gradually lead into the abstract discussion — this makes it much
easier for the reader to understand. For two reasons, an (apparently) abstract
question benefits from being anchored to reality:

- **Engaging the real world deepens your thinking about the problem.** Because the
  real world is complex and its elements are interrelated, it supplies your topic
  with many sources of inspiration and substance. Sometimes, when your topic touches
  on too many things at once, anchoring it to one or two concrete cases can make an
  otherwise complex and vague topic clearer and more intelligible.
- **Engaging the real world quickly captures the reader's attention.** Think about
  it: during a PhD application, the professor you "cold-email" receives a barrage of
  messages every day — does he have the patience to read every letter carefully? Our
  research topics are sometimes highly specialised and abstract; even for fellow
  scholars, one field apart is like a mountain apart. Connecting the problem to
  reality helps readers empathise.

Construction and planning design is a highly practice-oriented field. Your values
and findings will, very quickly, become visible in the physical world through the
implementation of design schemes. So, compared with philosophy, mathematics, physics,
astronomy, literature, and so on, our topics need more connection to the real world.
One distinctive feature of my own faculty — the Faculty of Architecture at TU
Delft — is that its doctoral research (as well as its master's design courses) has a
strong relationship to space, morphology, and design.

## 4. Interdisciplinarity

Architecture and planning is an applied, practice-oriented discipline. Many of the
concepts we use, and many of the methods we adopt, are borrowed from other "upstream"
disciplines — for example, the concept of *resilience* from ecology, *assemblage
thinking* from philosophy, and many research methods from the human and natural
sciences.

This makes **interdisciplinarity** especially important in our field. So when
choosing a doctoral topic, it is worth incorporating more content from other
disciplines. A larger span across disciplines often yields novel findings.
