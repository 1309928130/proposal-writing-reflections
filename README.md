# Reflections on Supervising Research-Proposal Writing

A short, practical guide to what makes a good PhD **research proposal (RP)** — and,
by extension, a good research question — in architecture, planning, and landscape.

These three essays were originally written in Chinese on Zhihu (December 2022),
drawing on my experience coaching students through overseas master's theses,
master's applications, and PhD applications. They are translated here into English
for use as teaching material on research methodology and proposal supervision.

## Summary

**[Part 1 — Applied vs. research thinking](01-applied-vs-research-thinking.md)**

- The undergraduate / master's / PhD distinction, by metaphor: the teacher gives you
  a gun and points at a bird (BA); points at the bird but you build the gun (MA);
  you find the bird and build the gun (PhD). Undergraduates do *applied* work with
  established knowledge; doctoral students produce *new* knowledge.
- Because design fields are highly applied, students rush to solutions before
  defining the problem — so design studios tend to be organised by building type
  (*typology*), whereas research-oriented teaching is organised around finding and
  solving problems (*morphology-* or better, *problem-oriented*).
- **The central claim: ask "WHY", not just "HOW" or "WHAT."** The research question —
  what it is, why it matters, why the problem arises, what exists already, why it is
  insufficient, and why *you* can address it — governs everything. The common
  failure is over-describing the *method* (HOW) and under-describing the *problem*
  (WHY). A second failure is forcing a cross-disciplinary topic from two bodies of
  existing knowledge, which does not constitute new knowledge.

**[Part 2 — Real-world relevance and interdisciplinarity](02-real-world-and-interdisciplinarity.md)**

- **Anchor abstract questions to the real world.** It deepens thinking (complexity
  and interrelation supply inspiration and substance), and it captures the reader's
  attention (a busy professor skimming cold emails). Anchor a sprawling topic to one
  or two concrete cases to make it clear.
- Design and planning are practice-oriented, so our topics must connect to the real
  world more than those in philosophy or mathematics do. (TU Delft's Faculty of
  Architecture is distinctive precisely in tying research to space, morphology, and
  design.)
- **Interdisciplinarity matters** because our concepts and methods are borrowed from
  "upstream" fields (e.g. *resilience* from ecology, *assemblage thinking* from
  philosophy). A larger disciplinary span often yields novel findings.

**[Part 3 — Let it be unfinished, and beware quantitative overreach](03-not-too-polished-and-quantitative-caution.md)**

- **The proposal should not be *too* polished.** An over-mature, keyword-stuffed RP
  invites the question: did you write this yourself? A student who cannot speak
  concretely about their own keywords or methods is exposed.
- **A caution on quantitative research.** Quantitative work has a clear paradigm and
  looks rigorous, but excessive quantitative research that advances neither theory
  nor practice is boring and useless. Under-studied, harder-to-quantify questions
  (quality of place, psychological experience of space, wayfinding cognition) are
  often more meaningful than well-worn, easily-measured indicators (density, street
  layout, accessibility).

## Why this matters for teaching

These reflections form the basis of how I supervise research and proposal writing:
**problem before method, relevance before abstraction, and honesty about one's own
work.** They are the same questions I put to students in studio reviews and thesis
supervision, and they translate directly into mentorship of research proposals at
master's and doctoral level.
