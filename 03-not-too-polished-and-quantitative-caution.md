## 5. The proposal should not be *too* polished

This view may be somewhat contentious, and I am not certain my judgement is entirely
correct.

In coaching, I once worked with a student applying to an architecture PhD from a
different discipline. By the time she came to me, I was presented with an already
very professional and mature document, full of keywords from our field. On asking, I
learned that her RP had indeed already been coached once by another doctoral
student before she approached me. I think in such a situation the professor will ask
the same question: **did you write this yourself? Can you talk specifically about a
certain keyword, or the method you used?** At the time this student was still in the
application process, and I looked forward to her progress.

## 6. A recent observation

It seems that, at present, quantitative research is more welcome than qualitative
research. Quantitative research has a relatively clear paradigm and involves a lot of
data (numbers), so it appears rigorous and is easier to publish. When there is an
excess of theory, letting the data speak is of course a good thing. But excessive
quantitative research — if it advances neither theory nor practice in any significant
way — is both boring and useless.

Recently I attended an academic conference in Barcelona (Urban Transitions 2022) and
exchanged views with fellow attendees in passing. I saw many studies that were
quantitative (I suspect many were master's students' output) with very limited
originality. Many of them were much the same: they took a few commonplace indicators
(often distilled from indicators in the existing literature, in a process that
already smacks of "chewing leftovers"), and then computed correlations back and
forth. For practice, such research offers no valuable insight; for academia, it
offers no methodological innovation.

My personal view is that, with quantitative research now so abundant, it would be
worth doing more qualitative research (or embedding more qualitative content within
one's research). When choosing a topic, a subject that incorporates something
difficult to quantify often becomes more challenging and more meaningful. Consider
urban design: some easily quantified things have been studied to death — density,
street layout, transport accessibility, visual accessibility, land-use mix, and so
on. Meanwhile, other important but relatively fuzzy content remains under-studied —
the quality of place, users' psychological experience of space, the cognition
involved in wayfinding, and so on.
