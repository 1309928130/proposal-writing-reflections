# What Does a Good Research Proposal Look Like? (Part 1)
## For PhD applications in architecture / planning / landscape

*Originally published in Chinese on Zhihu, December 2022.
Translated by the author.*

---

I received my master's degree from Tongji University and am currently pursuing a PhD
at one of the top QS-ranked architecture schools. Over the past few years I have
coached friends on their overseas master's theses, master's applications, and PhD
applications. Here I would like to draw on that experience to discuss what a good
PhD application Research Proposal (RP) should look like. I will explain by
comparing the differences I have seen between domestic and international teaching,
and between master's-level and doctoral research.

## Applied (design) thinking vs. research thinking

There is an intuitive metaphor for undergraduate, master's, and doctoral education.
For an undergraduate, the teacher hands you a gun, points at a bird, and tells you
to shoot it down. For a master's student, the teacher points out where the bird is,
and you must build the gun yourself to shoot it down. For a doctoral student, you
must find where the bird is yourself, and build the gun yourself to shoot it down.
Undergraduates learn general knowledge that already exists in textbooks; master's
students learn some existing frontier topics; doctoral students must develop new
knowledge themselves. This produces a pattern: the work undergraduates do is usually
applied — they use established, classic knowledge for practical tasks. The work
doctoral students do is usually research-oriented — they must stand on the shoulders
of giants and add to humanity's map of knowledge. Master's students sit somewhere in
between; a poor master's education resembles undergraduate study, while a good one
resembles doctoral work.

In my own field of architectural and planning design, the difference between
master's and doctoral work is even more pronounced. Because the design disciplines
are strongly applied (compared with the more "hardcore" fields of mathematics and
physics), designers are always eager to produce a design scheme, even when the
problem definition is not very clear. Moreover, because architectural design
involves such a mixture of professional and non-professional knowledge, producing a
workable scheme is already no small achievement. So people often do not demand a
rigorous definition of the initial problem.

Domestic and international architectural/planning education also differ in how they
emphasise applied versus research work. Domestic architecture undergraduate design
studios set their briefs by building type (typology) — from a café in first year, to
a kindergarten in third year, to a sports arena in fifth year, and so on — training
different building types and increasing design complexity step by step. International
design courses, by contrast, might offer a lecture series with several topics, and
students may choose any one of them to develop in depth. This process places more
emphasis on the ability to find and solve problems. In the end, the things students
choose to make may differ enormously in scale and type — from a façade shading-louvre
product design, to interior design, to a single building, to urban design and
regional planning. The faculty in my group call this course structure
"morphology-oriented" (though I personally feel "problem-oriented" or
"research-oriented" is more accurate).

## 2. Ask "WHY" more often, not "HOW" or "WHAT"

Having understood the differences between applied and research work, and some of the
deeper reasons behind them, one can think more clearly about how to build a
portfolio for a master's application and a research proposal for a PhD application.

Let us start with the PhD application. The most important element in a PhD
application is the research proposal (RP). Over a scholar's career, the RP reappears
in many forms at many stages — a master's proposal, a PhD application RP, a PhD
proposal, and a research grant application (with increasing depth in that order).

A research proposal is called a *proposal* because the research topic it describes is
still at an early stage, with much yet to be explored. A mature proposal typically
has to answer certain questions: what the research problem is, what existing
knowledge and tools exist (the shoulders of giants), and what one's own research
might contribute. But among all of these, the most important is the articulation of
the **research question** — the research question is the starting point of doctoral
research and the element that governs everything. What is this research question?
Why does it matter (WHY)? Why does this problem arise (WHY)? Who has already
researched around this question? Why is the existing research insufficient (WHY)?
Why am I able to do this research (WHY)? An interesting question with major
real-world significance can arouse a professor's strong interest even if its
research method is not yet determined.

One of the mistakes students commonly make is to discuss the research **method**
(HOW) at too great a length while under-developing the problem itself. Moreover, in
our field, many methods are borrowed ready-made from the natural and human sciences
and are not especially novel. Another mistake: after master's study, some students
have mastered the knowledge of a niche topic, and naturally want to cross it with
other knowledge in their own discipline, thereby producing a **plausible-sounding but
misguided research topic**. They little realise that what they learned at master's
level is often knowledge that already exists in the world. If simply crossing two
fields of existing knowledge were enough, then a PhD student would only need half a
year of reading to graduate rather than four years.

In a master's application, the most important element is the portfolio. A mistake
domestic undergraduates often make is that the storyline of the portfolio is
unclear. What problem is one's scheme trying to solve, and how does the design solve
it? Many students' schemes are solid in fundamentals — construction nodes, beam and
column layouts, circulation organisation (presenting WHAT), and may even adopt some
philosophical reflection or the values and methodology of some architectural school
(HOW) — yet the overall concept of the scheme arrives abruptly and feels forced.
The reason, again, is insufficient thinking about the problem (WHY).
